# Introduction

Automated conflict event coding has advanced substantially in recent years. Transformer-based models now perform well on tasks such as identifying actors, classifying event types, and recovering structured information from conflict narratives [@hu2022; @halterman2021]. Yet the extraction of fatality counts remains less well developed in the automation literature despite its importance for conflict measurement. Major conflict datasets apply fatality thresholds to determine which events qualify for inclusion and how they are classified. Fatality counts also provide the primary measure of conflict severity, used to track escalation, compare conflicts across time, and identify high-violence cases for closer analysis. 

Existing event datasets routinely record fatality counts, and automated systems sometimes attempt to extract them, but count fields have generally been treated as secondary outputs rather than as a research problem in their own right. For example, GDELT's V2COUNTS field remains widely used despite longstanding concerns about noise and validity [@leetaru2013], while more recent work in the abstractive event extraction literature incorporates fatality counts as one field among many rather than treating count extraction as the primary object of analysis [@simon2025]. As a result, conflict research now has a growing literature on automated coding of who acted, where, and in what form, but much less evidence on how well current models recover the fatality information used to measure conflict severity.

Count extraction has received less attention, and it is also difficult to evaluate with standard aggregate measures. In conflict event data, most observations are concentrated at the lower end of the count distribution, while high-fatality events are relatively uncommon. For example, in the GTD, more than half of recorded attacks result in no fatalities, while fewer than one in ten claim more than five lives [@lafree2007]. Under these conditions, aggregate performance metrics may obscure systematic error in the upper tail. A model that performs well on average may still systematically underpredict the high-fatality incidents that define escalation, peaks, and turning points in the conflict record. Recent evidence further suggests that naive large language model prompting for conflict fatality counts can be unstable and sensitive to query formulation, indicating a need for more systematic evaluation [@steinert2025].

This paper examines death count extraction using roughly 10,000 hand-coded incident summaries from the South Asia Terrorism Portal on the Maoist insurgency in India. The single-conflict design controls for actor identification, source language, and reporting conventions, so variation in model performance can be attributed directly to the quality of count extraction. My analysis compares two modeling approaches. The first uses fine-tuned sequence-to-sequence models, with Flan-T5-Large as the primary model and T5-XL-QLoRA as an upper-bound comparison. The second uses prompted large language models, with Llama-3.1-8B as the primary model and GPT-4o-mini as a benchmark. Both are evaluated against a ConfliBERT-Poisson baseline on the same held-out test set. The analysis proceeds in three stages. It first establishes baseline performance across model families. Next, it introduces a bin-level evaluation framework spanning `0`, `1`, `2`, `3-5`, and `6+` deaths to identify where aggregate metrics obscure performance differences. Finally, the paper tests a set of targeted interventions designed to improve performance on rare high-count cases.

The results show that generative models outperform the encoder baseline on aggregate measures, while the bin-level analysis reveals sharper differences on rare high-fatality cases. Training-side interventions on Flan-T5-Large do not close the gap on the highest count bin, pointing to a model capacity constraint rather than a data problem. Among the LLM prompting strategies, prompts that include worked examples substantially improve performance on the highest count bin, while clarifying the coding protocol alone may be counterproductive.

The bin-level framework introduced here offers a more diagnostic alternative to aggregate metrics, and future work on count extraction should adopt it alongside overall error measures. With regard to model selection, the results suggest that fine-tuned seq2seq models and prompted LLMs are not interchangeable. Seq2seq models perform well across the bulk of incidents but are limited on the upper tail by training data sparsity, while few-shot prompting with bin-balanced and hard-case examples substantially improves LLM performance on high-fatality cases. Researchers who incorporate automated count extraction into their work can expect reliable performance on low-count events, but high-fatality predictions warrant manual review.
