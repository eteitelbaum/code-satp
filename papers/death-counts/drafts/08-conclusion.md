# Conclusion

This paper has argued that automated death count extraction deserves to be treated as a primary research problem in conflict event coding rather than as a secondary question bundled into broader information extraction systems. Fatality counts are central to how conflict severity, escalation, and human costs are measured. The data are readily available, yet highly skewed. Most incident reports describe zero or one death, while the rarer high-fatality events carry disproportionate analytical weight. Under these conditions, an automated system can appear strong in aggregate while still failing on the very cases that matter most for substantive inference.

To address this gap, I evaluated death count extraction on a corpus of roughly 10,000 SATP incident summaries using two model families: fine-tuned seq2seq models and prompted instruction-tuned LLMs. Both tracks were compared to a ConfliBERT-Poisson baseline and evaluated on the same held-out test set. The central methodological contribution of the paper is the use of bin-level evaluation alongside aggregate metrics. By decomposing performance across the count distribution into bins of 0, 1, 2, 3--5, and 6+, the analysis reveals that strong performance on common low-count events does not imply reliable performance on rare, high-fatality incidents.

The baseline experiments show that generative approaches are clearly more effective than the encoder baseline for this task. Flan-T5-Large and GPT-4o-mini perform strongly overall, and most generative models achieve near-ceiling results on the low-count bins that dominate the test set. But the bin-level analysis complicates this favorable picture. Performance deteriorates sharply in bins 3--5 and especially 6+, and models that look broadly competitive in aggregate differ much more substantially once attention shifts to rare high-fatality cases. Case-level diagnostics further show that these failures are not all of the same kind. Some reflect irreducible limits in the source text or ground-truth labels, while others arise from recurring and potentially addressable problems, including ambiguity around claimed attacker deaths and errors in aggregating counts across multiple victim groups.

The intervention experiments clarify which of these rare-bin failures can be reduced and by what means. On the seq2seq side, reweighting and oversampling strategies do not improve high-count performance and often worsen overall MAE by pushing the model toward overprediction on ambiguous cases. Back-translation provides modest directional improvement on bin 3--5, but no training-side intervention closes the remaining gap on bin 6+. The results therefore suggest that the hardest seq2seq failures are primarily constrained by model capacity rather than by class imbalance alone. On the LLM side, the pattern is different. A protocol clarification instruction by itself is counterproductive, but few-shot prompting with targeted hard-case examples substantially improves performance. The combined prompt produces the strongest results for Llama-3.1-8B, bringing its rare-bin performance to a level comparable to GPT-4o-mini and, on point estimates, slightly above it.

Taken together, these findings have both methodological and practical implications for conflict research. Methodologically, work on automated extraction of count fields should not rely on aggregate error analysis alone but instead must reflect the shape of the underlying distribution and the substantive importance of rare events. Practically, the results suggest that automated death count extraction is already usable, especially when paired with generative models, but it should be deployed with attention to the special difficulty of high-fatality cases. A realistic workflow is therefore not fully automated replacement of human coding, but model-assisted extraction combined with targeted review of the relatively small number of high-count predictions where error is most consequential. More broadly, the paper shows that progress on conflict event count extraction depends not only on stronger models, but also on evaluation frameworks and intervention strategies that are explicitly designed for rare but analytically decisive events.
