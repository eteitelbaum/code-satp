
# Count Extraction and the Rare-Event Problem

Automated conflict event coding has made substantial progress on classification tasks such as identifying event types, coding actor dyads, and tagging locations. These tasks share a structure amenable to standard supervised learning: they involve a fixed label set, outputs that can be evaluated against a predefined ontology, and answers that can often be recovered as text spans. In contrast, extracting counts differs in several key ways that make it substantially harder. Consider the following passage from the dataset of Maoist conflict events used for this study:

> Two CRPF personnel were killed and four others injured during an encounter with cadres of the CPI-Maoist in Giridih District. The encounter followed an ambush by the Maoists on a Police patrol in the Madhuban forest area and it reportedly continued for over 12 hours. Police sources claimed that over a dozen Maoists were killed, but their colleagues managed to take away the bodies. Maoists also triggered seven landmine blasts during the encounter.

The passage does not yield a single unambiguous count. Two CRPF deaths are explicitly confirmed while "over a dozen Maoists" are claimed killed but their bodies were not recovered. A model, just like a human coder, must decide whether unconfirmed, unrecovered deaths count, and if so, how to interpret "over a dozen" as a point estimate. The answer requires the model to attribute the outcome to a group, handle uncertainty, and aggregate counts across clauses, before it even encounters the competing numeric expressions "four others injured" and "seven landmine blasts" that it must discard. Meanwhile, the exclusion of Maoist fatalities would require the model to record zero fatalities for one actor (Maoists) while maintaining the total (two) for the other actor (the police). Yet by including the Maoist fatalities the model would need to produce a count in the high-fatality tail of the training distribution which, as we show below, is the range where all models face the greatest challenge.

We can summarize the main challenges as follows:

1. *Unbounded output space.* The model must produce a non-negative integer over a potentially large range, including values that are rare or unseen in training;
2. *Non-verbatim answers.* The correct count is often not a single text span, but must be constructed from information distributed across clauses or conditioned on qualifiers;
3. *Zero discrimination.* Zero is a common and meaningful outcome even when numeric expressions are present, requiring the model to ignore irrelevant quantities (e.g., arrests, escapes, injuries);
4. *Outcome and actor attribution.* The model must distinguish between different types of events (killed, wounded, arrested, escaped) and correctly assign counts to actor categories;
5. *Uncertainty and ranges.* Reports frequently include ranges, qualified claims, or provisional outcomes, requiring the model to decide how to handle lower and upper bounds and whether to include uncertain or delayed information. 

A variety of approaches have been adopted for automated count extraction, each facing identifiable limits with respect to the challenges described above. Even the most capable generative models struggle to produce accurate counts for rare, high-fatality events, and no prior study has systematically examined whether targeted interventions can address this failure.

## Approaches to Count Extraction

Work on automated count extraction has progressed from early rule-based systems that locate numeric tokens near death-related keywords, through supervised encoder models trained on labeled examples, to the generative neural architectures that now represent the state of the art. Where earlier systems search for a number already present in the text, generative models produce a predicted count by reasoning over the full input. Recent work on structured event extraction from conflict reports has converged on generative architectures as the most capable approach for producing count outputs.

### Dictionary-Based Approaches

The earliest automated conflict coding systems relied on dictionary matching, where keyword co-occurrences trigger event codes and proximity to numeric tokens produces candidate counts [@schrodt1994]. For example, GDELT's V2COUNTS field extracts counts by associating number tokens with nearby death-related terms [@leetaru2013]. Dictionary-based count fields are widely understood to be unreliable. Overcounting is systematic because any number near a death keyword becomes a count candidate, aggregation across clauses is impossible, and zero cannot be represented because a zero-fatality event does not produce a number to extract.

### Sequence-to-Sequence Models

More recently, researchers have applied sequence-to-sequence models to structured information extraction from text, including count fields in conflict and crisis event datasets. Built on encoder-decoder architectures such as T5 and its instruction-fine-tuned variants [@chung2022], these models encode the full input bidirectionally and generate outputs autoregressively. Seq2seq models can aggregate partial counts, infer zero from the absence of fatality evidence, and produce counts even when the text is indirect. @simon2025 demonstrate this capability on UCDP data, where fine-tuned generative models achieve approximately 69% exact match on the Deaths Low field. 

The ceiling of seq2seq models is nonetheless bounded by arithmetic reasoning capacity. For example, a model fine-tuned on sufficient examples can learn that two soldiers and three militants sum to five, but its ability to handle longer, multi-group casualty chains scales with model size rather than training data volume. We should therefore expect a gap between smaller and larger seq2seq models on high-count cases that training-side interventions cannot easily close.

### Decoder-Only Large Language Models

Decoder-only large language models (LLMs) such as Llama and GPT-4o-mini offer a related but distinct approach to count extraction. These models generate responses conditioned on a natural language prompt without any encoding step. Decoder-only models bring two relevant capabilities. The first is knowledge learned during large-scale pretraining that can assist with context disambiguation. The second is in-context learning, or the ability to adapt responses based on examples provided in the prompt without updating its parameters [@brown2020]. 

Recent work shows that LLMs can perform count extraction accurately when counts are small, explicitly stated, and the distribution is not heavily skewed [@braun2025]. In practice, however, @steinert2025 shows how naive zero-shot prompting for conflict fatality counts can be unreliable and sensitive to how the question is framed, with estimates shifting with language choice in ways that do not track actual event severity. This suggests that LLM count extraction is not simply a matter of asking the right question but requires examples that demonstrate the full range of the target distribution, including the high-fatality tail that naive prompting systematically underrepresents.

### Encoder Models with a Regression Head

Another interesting avenue adapts pretrained encoder models for count prediction by appending a regression head to the encoder's text representation, producing a predicted count directly rather than generating it token by token.^[span-extraction] @zhong2023 apply this approach to victim count extraction for crisis events using a numeracy-augmented encoder, providing the closest prior precedent for conflict fatality prediction. While encoder models with regression heads have not been applied to conflict event coding, they can provide a useful architectural baseline against which to evaluate generative models.

^[span-extraction]: The extractive alternative treats count extraction as a span-selection problem, identifying a numeric token in the text as the answer, but this fails because the correct count is frequently not a verbatim span and may require aggregation across clauses or a zero output when no deaths are mentioned.

## Evaluation Gaps and Targeted Interventions

The approaches described above have been evaluated in isolation, on datasets with different distributional properties, and using aggregate metrics that obscure performance on rare high-count events. @simon2025 report an aggregate "deaths score" as the mean across six sub-fields, @zhong2023 evaluate overall extraction performance without breaking out results by count magnitude, and @semnani2025 report aggregate event extraction F1 that conflates count fields with categorical and boolean variables. No prior study systematically compares approaches on the same data, applies interventions targeting rare-count failure, or reports bin-level performance designed to expose systematic failure at the high end of the distribution.

The most recent work frames count extraction as one component of a broader abstractive event extraction agenda [@simon2025; @semnani2025]. These studies are oriented toward demonstrating system capabilities across multiple event fields simultaneously rather than optimizing performance on any single field or investigating the effect of targeted interventions. They establish that generative models can extract count information from conflict text but leave open the question of whether performance on rare high-count events can be meaningfully improved and at what cost.

This gap matters because conflict fatality distributions are heavily right-skewed, with most events recording zero or very few deaths and a small minority recording mass casualties. Standard evaluation metrics such as mean absolute error or root mean squared error are dominated by common low-count cases and can mask systematic failure at the high end. High-fatality events drive conflict severity indices, escalation assessments, and policy responses, and errors on rare but extreme cases are not marginal [@eck2012]. A model that performs well on zero and single-fatality events while failing consistently on events with ten or more deaths would appear competitive under standard evaluation but be inadequate for the cases that matter most for conflict measurement.

Two classes of intervention are available to address this. For seq2seq models, training-side strategies address the data imbalance directly by augmenting training examples through back-translation [@sennrich2016], oversampling rare-count events, or applying loss functions that penalize high-count errors more heavily. These interventions increase the model's exposure to hard cases but cannot compensate for architectural capacity constraints. For decoder LLMs, the analogous intervention is prompt engineering, specifically providing few-shot examples that demonstrate the full range of the output distribution. Theory predicts that examples are more effective than additional instructions because examples directly anchor the model to the output distribution rather than describing it abstractly [@brown2020; @wei2023]. We test both classes of intervention systematically below.
