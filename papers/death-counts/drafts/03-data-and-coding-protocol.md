# Data and Coding Protocol

The interventions described above are evaluated on incident reports from the South Asia Terrorism Portal (SATP), a publicly accessible repository of conflict event summaries for South Asia.^[South Asia Terrorism Portal, Institute for Conflict Management (www.satp.org)] I use incidents covering the Maoist (Naxalite) insurgency in India from 2005 to 2016, a period that captures the peak intensity of the conflict across central and eastern India. The full corpus contains approximately 9,919 incident reports involving security forces (CRPF, STF, CoBRA, and state police), CPI-Maoist cadres, and civilians.

The SATP corpus has been used extensively in both the conflict studies and NLP literatures, making it a well-validated foundation for this study's experiments. Moreover, a single-conflict corpus offers diagnostic advantages over multi-conflict datasets. In datasets covering many conflicts, a model error on a count field could stem from multiple causes that are difficult to disentangle, such as actor misidentification, inconsistent reporting conventions across conflicts, variation in source languages, or failures in count extraction itself. In the SATP corpus, actor identification is fixed across every incident, the language is consistently English, and the reporting conventions are drawn from a single source. When a model underpredicts a high-fatality incident, we can rule out these alternative explanations and attribute the failure specifically to how the model handles uncertainty in the source text.

In order to focus the analysis on incidents where fatality counts are relevant, I filtered the dataset to armed assault and bombing events, thereby excluding non-violent events such as surrenders, arrests, and abductions. This yielded a working corpus of approximately 4,300 incidents. Despite restricting to violent event types, the distribution of death counts remains highly skewed (see Figure 1).

Thirty-seven percent of incidents record zero deaths, 38% record exactly one, 12% record two, 9% fall in the three-to-five range, and only 4.4% record six or more fatalities. This skewed distribution poses a fundamental challenge for automated extraction because models trained on data where 75% of incidents record zero or one death have little basis for accurately predicting the rare but consequential high-count events.

The dataset records a fatality count for each incident following the SATP source material, coding all deaths mentioned in the incident report as well as a breakdown by actor category.^[category-breakdown] This straightforward approach belies deeper epistemological questions related to the quality and veracity of conflict reportage. Some reported deaths are confirmed by physical evidence and independent sources, while others rest on unverifiable claims by one party to the conflict. Major conflict datasets feature different approaches to addressing such nuances. UCDP applies a conservative lower-bound standard, discounting single-source claims of combatant deaths [@sundberg2013], whereas ACLED records deaths as reported and codes the source separately, leaving the evidentiary judgment to the user [@raleigh2010]. The coding in this study is closer to the ACLED approach, recording what the source documents report without applying an independent evidentiary filter.

^[category-breakdown]:  This paper does not analyze the breakdown of fatalities by actor group (security forces, Maoist cadres, and civilians) but the per-side decomposition is a natural extension of this work.

For model training, I split the filtered corpus into training, validation, and test sets using a stratified random split that preserves the count bin distribution across all three partitions. The training set is comprised of approximately 2,580 incidents (60%), and the validation and test sets each consist of 859 incidents (20% each). The test set was held out throughout all model development, while the validation set was used to monitor training loss and implement early stopping for the seq2seq models and to evaluate the few-shot prompt interventions prior to final testing.
